\begin{abstract}
Long-horizon agents often terminate on stale, misattributed, or missing
evidence: the worker believes it is done because its operational memory
says so, not because the trace supports it. We introduce Agent Memory
Observatory, a supervisory meta-agent that audits a worker coding
agent's completion claims against its execution trace and can block
termination or issue bounded corrective guidance when the cited
evidence is insufficient. The supervisor is deliberately trace-only: it
judges whether a claim is \emph{justified} by evidence available at
claim time, a distinct question from whether the work is
\emph{correct}, which a hidden evaluator measures exactly once after
termination. We evaluate the supervision loop on matched held-out
fixture pairs (temporal freshness, source provenance, requirement
state) and four negative controls that price the supervisor's
false-block rate, across worker arms ranging from unsupervised to
halting-and-repairing supervision, with an oracle supervisor as an
upper bound. On the predeclared primary comparison, supervision reduced
accepted-unsupported completions from \RESULT{baseline rate} to
\RESULT{supervised rate} (\RESULT{test statistic}), with a false-block
rate of \RESULT{FPR} on supported controls. We report where the
supervisor fails --- over-blocking, intervention-induced liveness
failure, and the fresh-but-wrongly-grounded blind spot that only the
oracle closes --- and argue that supervisory verdicts must be
communicated as evidence audits, never as correctness guarantees.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Responsible-Use Statement}
% MANDATORY (desk-reject if omitted). Venue asks for "potential
% societal impacts". Drafted in full; keep under ~0.25 page in final.
Agent Memory Observatory is a control on agent overclaiming: it blocks
a worker agent from terminating on completion claims its own execution
trace does not support. We believe the primary societal impact is
positive --- fewer silently unverified ``done'' states in automated
software work --- but supervision of agents by agents carries risks of
its own, and this system is designed and reported to keep them visible.

\textbf{An allow is not a guarantee.} The supervisor audits
\emph{justification}, not \emph{correctness}: our own provenance
fixtures construct cases where fresh, well-cited evidence grounded in
the wrong source passes a trace-only audit while the hidden evaluator
fails the work. Deployments must not market supervisory approval as
verification of correctness, and our reported
supported-but-incorrect rates quantify exactly how far that gap
extends.

\textbf{Oversight displacement.} A visible gate invites humans to stop
checking. Because the supervisor is deterministic and rule-based, its
failure modes are enumerable, and we report them (false-block rate,
liveness failures, the provenance blind spot) alongside its catch rate;
we recommend surfacing supervisor verdicts to human operators as
evidence audits with cited events, not as pass/fail badges.

\textbf{Who supervises the supervisor.} Granting a meta-agent authority
to halt a worker creates a new failure surface: over-blocking can deny
service, and repair guidance can loop a worker into budget exhaustion.
Our evaluation prices these directly (negative controls for false
blocks; liveness analysis for induced non-termination), and we argue
any deployed supervisory gate should ship with equivalent
counter-metrics.

\textbf{Scope of release.} Experiments use self-hosted open-weight
models on fixture repositories; no human subjects, personal data, or
live production systems are involved. The released artifact contains
fixtures, protocols, and run records only.
